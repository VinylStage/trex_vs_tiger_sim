Before you start

The T-Rex vs Tiger Simulator explores one playground question with the Monte Carlo method: if N adult Tyrannosaurus rex met M adult tigers on an open plain, who would win? It has since grown into a tool for pitting many species against each other, from lions and wolves to elephants and mammoths. Instead of showing a single fight, it draws plausible but uncertain traits from the literature and from stated assumptions, simulates hundreds or thousands of battles with slightly different values each time, and summarises them as probabilities and distributions.

  • Nothing to install. A current browser (Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari) with JavaScript is all you need. It also works on phones, but heavy scenarios are much faster on a computer.
  • Everything is computed in your browser. The runs are spread across several Web Workers, matched to your device's CPU cores, and neither your settings nor your results are sent to a server. See the privacy policy for details.
  • The results are not a prediction. T. rex and tigers never lived at the same time, and every interaction rule (latching, shaking off, bleeding, morale) is an assumption. The point is to see how the answer changes when the assumptions change. The ideas behind the model are explained in How the model works.

The screen at a glance

The simulator has four parts. On a wide screen the settings and the results sit side by side; on a narrow screen such as a phone, the results follow below the settings.

  • Header: the title, a Copy settings link button that copies the current settings as a URL, the Tutorial button for the on-screen tour, and the language button (한국어 ↔ English). Switching keeps your results and settings and moves the address to the Korean (/play/) or English (/en/play/) version, so a bookmark or shared link opens in the same language.
  • Settings panel: the Advanced switch sits at the top. Off, you get Simple mode with presets and each side’s species and count; on, you get every setting of the free matchup or the classic model. The Run simulation button and its progress bar are pinned to the bottom of the panel.
  • Results panel: once a run finishes it shows the win shares and the verdict, what happened to each side, summary cards and a battle replay, plus four charts in the classic model.
  • Footer: a reminder that this is an educational model, plus links to the model document and the source code.

Simple mode: pick, run, read

Open the simulator without any settings in the link and you land in Simple mode. You choose the species and the numbers; everything else runs at its default. On a first visit, press Start the tour in the banner under the header, or the Tutorial button at any time, for a five-step tour of exactly this screen.

  1. Choose a match-up. A Presets button sets both species and counts at once and shows a line on why that match-up is there. To build your own, pick a species for side A and side B. A species may face its own kind.
  2. Set the numbers. Use the quick buttons (1 · 2 · 5 · 10 · 50 · 100 · 1,000) or type a count: up to 5,000 per side and 5,500 in total. For species that fight as a group (lion, wolf, spotted hyena, Homo erectus) the quick buttons stop at 100, because a large group’s morale tends to break all at once; type a number if you want more.
  3. Run. Every press of Run simulation draws a fresh seed and plays the match-up a few hundred times. Very large counts lower the number of runs to fit the work budget.
  4. Read the result. The win-share bar (side A blue, side B orange, draws grey) comes first, then a sentence naming the most common outcome with its 95% interval. At the end of a battle (mean) lists, for each side, how many were still standing, how many fled and how many were incapacitated on average; the three add up to the side’s count. The run count, the seed and the summary cards sit under Details.
  5. The rout note. When a group that shares morale lost, and in those losses 80% or more of it fled rather than being incapacitated, a note says its morale broke all at once. That is not a bug: each departure and each incapacitated ally lowered the others’ morale in a chain. The Why? link explains the rule.
  6. Share. Copy settings link copies an address holding the species, counts and seed. It reopens in Simple mode, and its first run reuses the seed, so whoever opens it sees the same numbers. Share result makes a result card image and a result page link (sharing and reproducing).

Runs, seed, time limit, the pack buff and the rest are hidden in Simple mode. To reach them, switch on Advanced.

Advanced settings manual

The Advanced switch at the top of the settings panel reveals every setting Simple mode keeps out of the way. Your species, counts and results stay where they are, and switching it off takes you back to Simple mode. Simple mode does not use values you changed under Advanced and says so when there are any. The Manual link next to the switch opens this section in a new tab.

Under the switch you choose between two models.

  • Free matchup: a symmetric model in which either side can be any species. Simple mode runs on it too. Everything from the free matchup form to the work budget describes this form.
  • Classic (large vs group): the original T. rex vs tiger model this site started with. With its editable range table and sensitivity chart it works as a lab for testing one assumption at a time (the classic lab).

Trait ranges: min, mode and max

Every animal fights with its species’ 20 individual traits, and no trait is a single number. Each is a triangular distribution given by min, mode and max: min and max bound the plausible values, and the mode is the most plausible one. Each run draws one value per trait as that run’s species average; with an individual variation (CV) above zero, the animals in that run then scatter around it. A wide range therefore shows up as a wide spread of outcomes: it is how “we are not sure about this number” enters the results.

Individual traits in the free model
Group Traits What they do
Body Mass (kg), body radius (m) Health is proportional to mass, and the mass ratio decides who can latch on and who knocks whom back. The radius enters collisions and strike reach.
Movement Turn rate, sprint speed, cruise speed, sprint endurance, recovery time Within 60 m of its target an animal sprints and spends stamina; exhausted, it backs off to recover. Turn rate decides who gets the other into its strike cone first.
Strikes Strike force (kN), lethality (HP/kN), strike interval, reach, arc, accuracy An animal strikes whatever is in its frontal cone at a fixed interval. A hit deals force × lethality, scaled by a random factor between 0.7 and 1.3.
Latching Latch chance, latch endurance, latch damage (HP/s) Used only by species that can latch, and only on a target heavy enough (three times its own mass by default). For every other species they are fixed at zero and never read.
Shaking off, bleeding Shake rate (1/s), bleed rate (1/s) How readily an animal throws off riders, and how fast the wounds this side inflicts keep draining the other side’s health.
Morale Morale (0–1), morale loss (0–1) Below 0.2 an animal flees. Bigger wounds cost more morale; morale loss is what a group animal gives up when a nearby ally is incapacitated.

Mass, speeds, strikes and the other per-animal traits take the individual variation; body radius, lethality, bleed rate and the pack-buff traits are drawn once per run. Most preset ranges rest on the same sources as the species tables on the parameters page. Traits the classic model never needed for a role, such as stamina for the large animals, are new assumptions.

Can I edit the ranges on screen? Not yet. The free form uses the chosen species’ preset ranges; a trait table is planned for the next round of the redesign. A settings code or link that carries edited ranges is already honoured, and the run uses its values. To change ranges one by one today, use the range table in the classic lab.

Pack buff

The pack buff is a separate layer on top of the individual model. It only exists for a social species with at least two on its side, and it switches on and off for each animal, not for the side as a whole. It never adds raw damage. What it changes is who attacks whom, from where, how often a grab succeeds and how long courage holds.

  • On when the species is social, at least one effect is switched on, the animal is not fleeing, and another ally is inside its cohesion radius (about 60 m by default for lions, wolves and hyenas, about 30 m for Homo erectus).
  • Off when no ally has been inside the radius for longer than the grace period (2 s by default), or when the animal flees or is incapacitated. A last survivor fights on its own traits.
  • Solitary species (tiger, brown bear, T. rex and others) never get it, however many there are. Crowding around one target makes them get in each other’s way instead, which lowers their accuracy and latch chance.
The five pack-buff effects
Switch What a buffed animal does
Coordinated approach Against a much heavier opponent, the group spreads out around its rear and steps out of its frontal strike cone. Animals approaching this way do not get in each other’s way.
Shared target Prefers the opponent allies are already fighting, and switches together when that opponent drops out.
Group latching The more allies already hold on, the likelier the next grab (up to twice the base chance), and the slower the held opponent turns.
Morale support and contagion Outnumbering the enemy nearby raises courage, being outnumbered lowers it (by at most 0.1). A nearby ally being incapacitated or fleeing costs morale, with a cap on how much can go at once. With morale & routing off, this effect is off too.
Relief A tired rider lets go and hands its opponent to a fresh ally nearby instead of hanging on until exhausted.

The switches appear when either side is a social species and apply to both sides. With all of them off, a social species behaves exactly like a solitary one, so you can measure how much the buff matters by running the same match-up both ways. Each social species also carries six pack-buff traits (cohesion radius, group-latch gain, focus bonus, morale support, rout shock and relief stamina), drawn from their ranges every run. The Pack-buff rules disclosure in the form sums all of this up.

Global model constants

Five assumptions are shared by both sides. Like the traits, each is drawn from a min, mode and max every run; on screen they are fixed at their preset ranges for now.

Global model constants and their preset ranges
Constant Min / mode / max Meaning
Latch mass ratio 2 / 3 / 5 × A target must be at least this many times heavier to be latched onto.
Knock-back mass ratio 1.5 / 2 / 4 × A hit only sends the target reeling back to recover when the striker is at least this many times heavier.
Wound reference 0.05 / 0.1 / 0.2 Losing this share of maximum health in one blow costs the full wound-morale penalty; smaller wounds cost proportionally less.
Crowding interference 0 / 0.1 / 0.3 How much each extra unbuffed ally on the same target cuts accuracy and latch chance.
Buff grace period 0 / 2 / 5 s How long an animal outside the cohesion radius keeps its buff.

Shared settings

Shared settings of the free form
Setting Range (default) What it does
Monte Carlo runs 1–20,000 (500) How often the match-up is repeated. More runs narrow the 95% interval: near a 50% share, 500 runs give about ±4.4 percentage points.
Seed 0–4,294,967,295 (20261019) Where the random numbers start. The same seed with the same settings gives exactly the same results. Under Advanced the seed stays until you change it; Simple mode draws a new one for every run.
Arena side 100–5,000 m (600 m) A square arena. An animal that flees across the edge leaves the battle.
Time limit 30–3,600 s (1,200 s) A side wins the moment the other has no fighter left. If that has not happened by the time limit, or both sides run out at the same moment, it is a draw.
Individual variation (CV) 0–0.3 (0.08) The coefficient of variation between animals within one run. At 0 every animal uses that run’s values exactly.

Morale & routing On (default) On: an animal on either side whose morale falls below 0.2 flees. Off: nobody flees, everyone fights until incapacitated, and the pack buff’s morale effect is off as well.

Work budget

So that a single shared link can never freeze a browser, the amount of work per batch is capped. Work is runs × (time limit ÷ 0.1 s) × both sides’ total count, and a setup in which the pack buff can act (a social species with any effect on) counts 2.5 times, because it is heavier to compute. Above 5×10¹⁰ the number of runs is lowered to fit, and the runs field shows the lowered value.

Take 1,000 wolves against 1,000 tigers at the default 1,200 s limit: one run is 12,000 steps × 2,000 animals × 2.5 = 60 million, so at most 833 runs fit. A shorter limit or smaller counts leave room for more. In Simple mode, shrinking the counts again gives you the full run count back.

The classic lab

Choosing Classic (large vs group) opens the site’s original model. One large animal species that fights alone (T. rex, elephant, buffalo, brown bear, mammoth, Homo erectus) faces one group animal species that attacks in numbers (tiger, lion, wolf, hyena, Homo erectus), and the two roles follow different rules. It is a different model from the free matchup, so the same pairing can give different numbers.

  • Nine matchup presets and counts of 1–500 large and 1–5,000 group animals, listed in classic step 1.
  • Scenario settings: runs, time limit, arena, individual variation, sampling, seed (with a Random button), group tactic (flank or direct) and morale. Here morale and fleeing apply to the group side only. See classic step 2.
  • The range table: all 22 parameters (11 per role) editable as min, mode and max (classic step 3).
  • Results: verdict, summary cards with the exchange ratio, four charts (outcomes, duration, survivors, sensitivity) and the replay (classic step 5).
  • Export · import settings: copy a code, save or load a .json file, or paste a code or link. Pasting a free code (TVT3-) opens it in the free form.

The classic screen has its own step-by-step tour of every input and chart. Under Advanced, the header’s Tutorial button switches to classic and starts it, and it can run a quick 100-run demo so the result steps have something to show.

Reading the result cards

A free-matchup result under Advanced opens with the verdict sentence and its 95% interval, the rout note when it applies, a reminder of the model’s limits and the run’s basis (sides, runs, seed, compute time). The banner saying the free model is still being calibrated is explained below. The cards that follow:

Free-matchup result cards
Card How to read it
Side A wins, side B wins, draw The share of runs with that outcome and its Wilson 95% confidence interval. The interval covers the sampling error of a finite number of runs, not whether the assumptions are right.
Battle duration (median) The median time until the battle ended, with its 5–95% range.
Still standing, fled, incapacitated (mean) Per side, the average number still fighting at the end, gone, and incapacitated, each with its share of the side. They add up to the side’s count, and the winning side can lose some of its own too.
Pack buff active (mean) Only for a buffed social side of two or more. The large number is the average share of animal-time spent buffed; the small print is the median time during which at least one animal had the buff.

The rout note appears only when the side that won fewer runs shares morale (a social species of two or more, with morale & routing and the morale-support switch on) and, in the runs it lost, at least 80% of it fled rather than being incapacitated. A solitary species never gets the note: each animal decides to flee on its own, so there is no group morale to break.

The replay re-simulates one run with its seed. The representative battle is, among the runs with the most common outcome, the one whose duration is closest to the median; Random battle picks any run. Animals with a body radius of 1 m or more are triangles pointing where they face, smaller ones are dots; side A is blue and side B orange. A thin ring marks a buffed animal, grey one that fled, and a cross one that is incapacitated. The shapes are symbols for position and state, not pictures of animals.

The free matchup has no charts or sensitivity analysis yet. To see which assumption drives a result, use the charts and sensitivity chart of the classic lab.

Sharing and reproducing

  • Settings links. Under Advanced, every change is written into the address (#s=…), and Copy settings link copies it. A link made under Advanced opens the free form; a classic link opens classic. Only links made in Simple mode reopen in Simple mode.
  • Settings codes and files. Copy settings code gives a TVT3- code in the free form and a TVT2- code in classic. Paste a code or link, or load a .json file, under Export · import settings on the classic screen; you see a preview before it is applied.
  • Result links. Share result below a result makes a /r/ link with a snapshot of the numbers and the settings. The result page saves a story (9:16) or square (1:1) card image, shares a text summary, and offers Run it yourself.
  • Seeds. Links carry the seed, so the same browser engine (Chrome and Edge, say) reproduces the result exactly. A different engine, such as Safari, may let single battles diverge, but the win shares agree statistically.
  • Model versions. A free link records the model version, so after a model change an old link still runs the version it was made with. A link from a newer version than the page you have opens the defaults, with a note that reloading fetches the newest version.

A simulator opened with Run it yourself checks your run against the shared snapshot, as long as you run it without changing anything, and reports one of the following.

Reproduction check messages
Message Meaning
Exactly the same Every number matches the snapshot, usually because the browser engine matches.
Reproduced The shared win shares fall inside this run’s 95% confidence intervals.
Differs Some shared win shares fall outside them. Try again with more runs before reading much into it.
Not checked The settings were changed before the run, so there is nothing to compare.

Model limits and provisional calibration

The free model is still being calibrated. Until a report compares its win shares with the classic model on every match-up both can express, read its numbers as provisional. If calibration changes the preset ranges, the model version goes up and old links keep reproducing under their own version.

  • Results follow from assumptions; they are not predictions. Many of these species never met, and the interaction rules (latching, knock-back, bleeding, morale, the pack buff) and a good share of the ranges are estimates rather than measurements.
  • One species per side, on a flat arena with no cover. Terrain, hiding places, the motive of a hunt or a carcass, and herd defence (elephants, buffalo or mammoths guarding their young) are not modelled yet.
  • Animals follow simple rules; they do not learn or change plans. The pack buff, too, is a handful of rules distilled from observed hunting behaviour.
  • The 95% intervals reflect the number of runs only, not the chance that a range itself is wrong.

For the reasoning and the full list, see the limitations on the model page.

Classic step 1: Pick the species or a matchup preset

Steps 1 to 7 cover the classic lab. To get there, switch on Advanced and choose Classic (large vs group).

At the top of the scenario panel, choose the species of the large animal (the side that fights alone) and of the pack animal (the side that attacks in numbers). Changing a species resets that side’s parameter ranges to the species’ presets; if you had edited them, a notice with an Undo button appears for a few seconds. The badge below tells you whether the match-up has been seen in the wild or the species never met.

The easiest way to start is a matchup preset. A preset sets both species, the head counts and the number of runs, and resets the parameter ranges to those species’ presets. Other settings, such as the seed, tactic and morale, stay as they are.

Classic step 2: Set up the scenario

The fields below the presets let you fine-tune the scenario. A value outside the allowed range is moved to the nearest allowed value when you leave the field. Every setting's default and range is listed in the scenario settings of the parameter reference.

T-Rex count · Tiger count
Head count on each side: 1–500 T-Rex and 1–5,000 tigers.
Monte Carlo runs
How many times the scenario is repeated. More runs give narrower confidence intervals but take longer. The default of 500 is a good start.
Time limit (s)
If neither side has won by then, the battle counts as a draw. Default: 1,200 s (20 min).
Arena side (m)
Size of the square arena. A routed tiger leaves the battle as soon as it crosses the edge.
Individual variation (CV)
Makes animals within the same run differ a little. At 0, every animal uses exactly the run's values.
Sampling
LHS (Latin hypercube) covers the parameter space more evenly for the same number of runs. Keep the default unless you have a reason not to.
Tiger tactic
Flank avoids the jaws and closes in from the side or rear; Direct charge runs straight at the nearest T-Rex. Tigers normally hunt alone, so both tactics are hypothetical.
Seed
The starting point of the random numbers. The same seed and settings reproduce the same results. The Random button draws a new seed, giving a fresh sample under the same assumptions.
Morale & routing
When on, tigers lose morale when they are bitten or when a nearby tiger is incapacitated, and flee once morale falls below 0.2. When off, tigers fight until incapacitated.

Classic step 3: Adjust parameter ranges

Open Advanced: model parameter ranges to see 22 parameters, 11 for the T-Rex and 11 for the tigers. Each parameter is not a single number but a triangular distribution defined by min · mode · max. Every run draws one value from it, so a wider range feeds more “we do not really know this” uncertainty into the results.

  • The order min ≤ mode ≤ max is kept automatically. If you type a min above the mode, the mode and max move up with it. When you shift a range a long way, enter min, then mode, then max.
  • A parameter that differs from its default gets a dot (•), and a badge counts the changes.
  • The ↺ button on each row resets that parameter; Reset all parameters below the table restores all 22 default ranges.
  • To fix a parameter to one value, enter the same number for min, mode and max. The run-to-run uncertainty disappears, although individual variation (CV), if on, still makes animals differ.

What each parameter does in the engine, and where its default range comes from, is listed one by one in the parameter reference.

Classic step 4: Run and follow progress

  1. Press Run simulation. The button is pinned to the bottom of the settings panel, so it stays within reach while you scroll.
  2. The progress bar shows finished runs, a percentage and an estimate of the time left, along with the number of workers computing in parallel.
  3. Press Cancel to stop. Work in progress is discarded; results you saw before stay on screen.

Heavy settings trigger a notice before you run:

  • Heavy scenario: a yellow notice appears when the estimated time exceeds about one minute, and a red warning above ten minutes. The estimate is rough; real time depends on your device and on how the battles unfold.
  • Work budget: if runs × (time limit ÷ 0.1 s) × total head count exceeds 5×10¹⁰ agent-steps, the number of runs is lowered to fit. This safety net stops a shared link from freezing a browser.

Classic step 5: Read the results

When the runs finish, the top of the results panel states the most likely outcome in one sentence, for example “T-Rex win probability 100.0% (95% CI 99.2%–100.0%)”. The line below records the head counts, number of runs, seed and computing time, so you always know which conditions produced the numbers.

Summary cards

What each summary card shows
Card Meaning
T-Rex wins · Tigers win · Draw The share of runs with each outcome, with a Wilson 95% confidence interval. The interval covers sampling error from running a finite number of battles; it says nothing about whether the model's assumptions are right.
Battle duration (median) The median time until the battle ended, with the 5–95% range.
T-Rex survivors (mean) The average number of T-Rex still alive at the end.
Tigers incapacitated · Tigers routed (mean) The average number of tigers incapacitated, and of tigers that fled or left the arena.
Exchange ratio Over all runs, tigers incapacitated ÷ T-Rex lost. It shows ∞ when no T-Rex was incapacitated and “—” when nobody was incapacitated.

Charts

  • Outcome probability with 95% CI: bars for the three outcomes, with the confidence interval as error bars.
  • Battle duration distribution: a histogram of how long battles lasted. Two peaks hint that different kinds of battle are mixed together, such as a quick rout and a long war of attrition.
  • T-Rex survivors distribution: how many T-Rex were left standing.
  • Sensitivity (Spearman correlation, top 10): the rank correlation ρ between each parameter's value and the “T-Rex advantage”. A bar to the right (positive) means higher values favour the T-Rex; to the left (negative), the tigers. Longer bars mark the assumptions the result depends on most.

Reading the sensitivity chart. The T-Rex advantage is the T-Rex share of HP left minus the share of tigers still fighting, a number between −1 and 1. With 500 runs, a |ρ| below about 0.1 is hard to tell apart from chance. The correlation looks at one parameter at a time and only captures monotonic trends, so effects that need two parameters to act together do not show up.

If you change a setting after a run, a “Settings changed” banner appears. The results on screen belong to the previous settings; run again to see the new ones.

Classic step 6: Watch a battle replay

Below the results you can watch one of the computed battles as an animation. The replay re-computes that battle with the same seed, so it unfolds exactly as the battle that was counted in the statistics.

  • Representative battle: the battle whose T-Rex advantage is closest to the median of all runs. It loads and plays automatically after a run, and shows what a “typical” battle looks like.
  • Random battle: picks one of the runs at random. Press it a few times to see how differently battles can unfold under the same settings.
  • Controls: play and pause, playback speed, the view (follow the fight automatically, the whole arena, or zoomed in on the T-Rex) and a position slider.
  • Legend: the legend under the canvas explains the symbols for the T-Rex (colour shows HP left) and for each tiger state: approaching, latched, recovering, routed and incapacitated.

The shapes in the replay are simple markers for position and state; they do not attempt to depict what the animals looked like.

Classic step 7: Share a link

Every change you make is written to the end of the URL in the address bar (#s=…). Copy settings link puts that URL on your clipboard; whoever opens it gets exactly your settings.

  • The link includes the seed, so in the same browser engine (Chrome and Edge both use V8) the results reproduce exactly. In a different engine, such as Safari, tiny rounding differences in maths functions can make individual battles diverge, but the probabilities and distributions agree statistically.
  • Only settings that differ from the defaults are stored, which keeps links short. With all defaults, the #s= part disappears.
  • Browsers never send the part after # (the fragment) to the server, so your settings are visible only to people who have the link.
  • The results themselves are not in the link. The recipient presses Run simulation and computes the same results in their own browser.

Tutorial: three experiments

Follow these experiments in order and in about ten minutes you will know how to use the simulator to explore how assumptions shape the answer. Each experiment changes one thing from the previous one. Exact numbers vary a little with device, browser and seed.

The experiments use the classic lab. If you prefer to be guided on screen, start the interactive classic tour. It highlights each input and result chart in turn and can run a quick 100-run demo. The Tutorial button in the header opens the short Simple-mode tour while you are in Simple mode, and this classic tour while you are under Advanced.

Experiment 1. Run the default scenario

  1. Open the simulator, switch on Advanced, choose Classic, and press the 10 T-Rex vs 100 tigers (default) preset.
  2. Press Run simulation and wait. It usually takes a few seconds to a few tens of seconds.
  3. Read the verdict and the cards. Under the default assumptions the T-Rex win almost every run, battles usually end in about half a minute, and most tigers flee rather than fall.
  4. Look at the two or three longest bars in the sensitivity chart. In preliminary runs, T-Rex bite force and tiger morale loss came out on top.

Experiment 2. Switch morale off

  1. Untick Morale & routing. The results panel shows the “Settings changed” banner.
  2. Run again.
  3. The duration histogram moves far to the right: tigers no longer flee, battles last minutes, and far more tigers are incapacitated. The T-Rex still win almost every time.
  4. The sensitivity ranking changes too. In a fight without retreat, the tiger damage rate (tiger.dps) becomes the most important assumption.

Experiment 3. What would it take for the tigers to win?

  1. With morale still off, open Advanced: model parameter ranges.
  2. Set the tiger's Damage per second to min 20, mode 40, max 80, roughly seven times the default (2, 6, 12).
  3. Run again. In a preliminary check, this one change let the tigers win close to half the runs.
  4. Now switch morale back on and run once more. With the same damage rate, the tigers flee early and the T-Rex win almost every time: the answer depends on a combination of assumptions, not on any single one.
  5. When you find an interesting setup, save it with Copy settings link or send it to a friend.

More ideas: raise the tiger count to 300 or 1,000, switch the tactic to direct charge, widen the bleed rate to 0.01–0.04, or set individual variation (CV) to 0 and see how much narrower the distributions get. Why these changes matter is explained in “What would make the tigers win?” on the model page.

Frequently asked questions

Why do the T-Rex almost always win?

Under the default assumptions a T-Rex weighs about 8 tonnes, more than 40 times a 180 kg tiger, and hit points scale with body mass. One good bite incapacitates most tigers, while a tiger's attacks are assumed to cause shallow wounds worth a few HP per second. With the morale model on, tigers that see others fall also flee quickly. This conclusion comes from the assumptions, so changing them changes the answer; see which assumptions would let the tigers win.

A friend opened my link and got slightly different numbers.

In the same browser engine the results should match exactly. Different engines, such as Chrome (V8) and Safari (JavaScriptCore), may round the last digit of trigonometric and logarithm functions differently, so individual battles can diverge while the probabilities and distributions stay statistically the same. A different number of runs changes the whole sampling design, so also check that the link was opened unchanged.

How many runs should I use?

The width of a confidence interval shrinks roughly with the square root of the number of runs. Near a probability of 50%, 500 runs give about ±4.4 percentage points and 2,000 runs about ±2.2. Explore with few runs, then raise the count for the result you want to keep.

It is very slow on my phone.

Phones have fewer and slower CPU cores, so the same scenario takes longer. Try 100–200 runs, or start with a preset that has fewer animals. Turning off the screen or switching apps during a run can also make the browser slow the work down.

When does a draw happen?

A battle is a draw when the time limit passes while at least one T-Rex is alive and at least one tiger is still fighting. It mostly happens with morale off and very many tigers, or with a short time limit.

Are my settings or results stored anywhere?

No. The settings live only in the part of the URL after #, and the results stay in your browser's memory until you close the tab. The only thing the site keeps in your browser is a flag that stops the tutorial offer from reappearing. See the privacy policy.

Troubleshooting

Symptoms and what to do
Symptom What to do
The page is blank or says it needs JavaScript. Enable JavaScript and check that no extension is blocking the site's scripts. If your browser is old, update it.
Progress does not move after pressing Run. Progress advances as each run finishes, so with very many animals the first step can take a while. If nothing happens for a long time, cancel and reduce the runs or the head counts.
The number of runs drops below what I typed. That is the work budget. A shorter time limit or fewer animals allows more runs.
A shared link opens with the default settings. The link may have been cut off, or the part after #s= damaged. Damaged values are ignored safely and the defaults are used. Copy the whole link again.
The replay is not moving. The replay stops at the end; press play to watch it again from the start. It also pauses drawing while it is off screen or the tab is in the background, to save battery.
