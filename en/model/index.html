How the model works: a Monte Carlo, agent-based battle model

A plain-language tour of how the simulator computes one battle and how it turns many battles into probabilities and statistics. Formulas and constants match the engine code, and every assumption comes with its limits.

At a glance

  • Agent-based: every T-Rex and every tiger is an individual agent with its own position, health, stamina and morale. Every 0.1 seconds each one decides and acts by fixed rules.
  • Monte Carlo: 22 traits that nobody knows exactly are drawn afresh from a range for every run. The same scenario is repeated hundreds or thousands of times and the outcomes are counted.
  • Two levels of uncertainty: per run, “we do not know the species' true value”; per animal, “individuals of the same species differ”.
  • The answer is a function of the assumptions: with the defaults, 10 T-Rex beat 100 tigers almost every time. Switch morale off and greatly raise the tigers' damage or numbers, and the result can flip. Showing that sensitivity is the whole point.

The question and a disclaimer

The simulator starts from a playground question: if N Tyrannosaurus rex met M tigers on an open field, who would win? There is no true answer. Instead, the model lays out every assumption needed to reach an answer and shows how the answer moves when those assumptions move.

  • T. rex lived in the Late Cretaceous, about 68–66 million years ago; tigers appear in the Pleistocene. The two never coexisted, and no such fight ever happened.
  • Every interaction rule (latching, shaking off, bleeding, morale, group tactics) is an assumption. The parameter reference marks which values are sourced and which are speculative.
  • Tigers are solitary ambush hunters. Coordinated attacks by several adult tigers on large prey are not documented, so both tiger tactics in the model are hypothetical.
  • The win probabilities on screen are what the model produces under its assumptions. Do not read them as predictions about real animals.

What is the Monte Carlo method?

When an answer is hard to derive with a single formula, the Monte Carlo method repeats a randomised experiment many times and studies the distribution of outcomes. It is named after the casino district of Monaco.

Here, one experiment is one battle. For every run the simulator draws new values, such as T-Rex bite force or the tiger's latch chance, from preset ranges, then simulates the battle from start to finish with those values. If the T-Rex win 480 of 500 runs, the estimated T-Rex win probability is 96%. More runs make the estimate steadier, and a confidence interval shows how precise it is.

Importantly, this probability mixes two things: luck within a fight, and our ignorance of the true traits. Narrow or widen a range and the probability changes with it.

Two levels of uncertainty

Level 1: per run (values we do not know)

Estimates of T-Rex bite force, for example, range from about 8.5 kN to 57 kN depending on the study. So each parameter is not one number but a triangular distribution defined by a minimum, a mode (the most plausible value) and a maximum. Values near the mode are drawn most often and values near either end more rarely. All 22 parameters have such a distribution, and every run draws one value per parameter.

You can choose how the values are drawn:

  • LHS (Latin hypercube sampling, default): each parameter's 0–1 range is cut into as many equal strata as there are runs; every stratum is sampled exactly once and the order is shuffled. Even a small number of runs reaches both ends of every range. Each parameter is shuffled independently, so correlations between parameters are not controlled.
  • Simple random: every value is drawn independently. With enough runs it matches LHS; with few runs it is lumpier.

Either way, a number u between 0 and 1 is turned into a value with the inverse cumulative distribution of the triangle. With minimum a, mode c and maximum b:

if u < (c−a)/(b−a): x = a + √(u·(b−a)·(c−a)); otherwise x = b − √((1−u)·(b−a)·(b−c))

Level 2: per animal (individuals differ)

Animals within a run are not identical. For every parameter with individual variation, each animal gets its own multiplier f, drawn from a normal distribution with mean 1 and standard deviation CV (default 0.08) and redrawn if it falls outside 0.5–1.5 (after 16 failed tries, f = 1). The animal's value is “run value × f”, clamped to the parameter's bounds.

  • With CV = 0, every animal uses exactly the run value.
  • Three parameters are set per run only and shared by all animals: bite lethality, body radius and bleed rate.
  • A tiger's cruise speed is capped at the same tiger's sprint speed.

How one battle unfolds

Arena and starting positions

The arena is an open, obstacle-free square with side L (600 m by default). The two groups start the smaller of 200 m and 0.4 L apart. The T-Rex stand in a vertical line on the left, facing the tigers, at most 15 m apart. The tigers start scattered at random inside a disc on the right. With the defaults, the groups start about 200 m apart.

Time in steps of 0.1 seconds

The engine advances time in 0.1 s steps (ticks). Each tick runs in this order:

  1. Rebuild a spatial grid so nearby animals can be found quickly.
  2. For every T-Rex, count the tigers latched onto it and their total mass.
  3. Each live T-Rex in turn picks a target, turns, moves, bites and takes bleeding damage.
  4. Each tiger in turn checks whether it routs, then acts according to its state.
  5. T-Rex with zero or negative health die, and the outcome checks run.

A tiger leaves the battle the moment it dies; a T-Rex is marked dead at the end of the tick. Animals are updated in index order within a tick, so the order can matter very slightly.

Health, damage and bleeding

  • Hit points (HP): for both species, 1 HP per kilogram of body mass. An 8,000 kg T-Rex starts with 8,000 HP and a 180 kg tiger with 180 HP. This is a modelling convention to put damage on one scale, not a law of physiology.
  • T-Rex bite: one hit deals bite force (kN) × bite lethality (HP/kN) × U(0.7, 1.3), where U(0.7, 1.3) is a uniform random factor. At the default modes that is 196–364 HP, which usually kills a tiger in one bite.
  • Tiger attacks: while latched, a tiger deals “damage per second × 0.1” every tick. The damage is also added to the T-Rex's accumulated wound total.
  • Bleeding: every second, a live T-Rex loses “accumulated wound × bleed rate” HP. With a bleed rate of 0.002/s, a T-Rex that has taken 1,000 HP of tiger damage loses 2 HP every second from then on. Wounds never close, and bleeding does not add to the wound total.

Bleeding matters most in long, no-morale battles: T seconds after taking wound W, bleeding has removed roughly W × bleed rate × T more. With W = 1,000 HP, 0.002/s and T = 300 s, that is about 600 HP.

T-Rex behaviour

Targeting and movement

  • A T-Rex targets the nearest tiger that is not latched onto anyone and is neither dead nor gone. Routed and recovering tigers can be targets.
  • It re-targets every 0.5 s, and at once when its target latches onto someone or dies.
  • Its heading changes by at most “turn rate × 0.1” per tick: the huge body's rotational inertia is assumed to make it a slow turner.
  • It only moves forward while the target is farther than 80% of its bite reach.
  • Latched tigers slow it down: speed is multiplied by max(0.3, 1 − total latched tiger mass ÷ own mass). Five 180 kg tigers on an 8,000 kg T-Rex give 0.89.
  • Speed is halved while the heading is more than 60° off the target.

Biting

When its bite cooldown has expired, a T-Rex looks for a target inside a frontal cone: within “bite reach + 0.8 m (tiger radius)” of its centre and within “bite arc” of straight ahead. The bite arc is a half-angle, so the full cone is twice as wide.

  • Tigers latched onto this T-Rex come first; otherwise the nearest candidate is chosen.
  • With no candidate, it tries again next tick.
  • An attempt restarts the cooldown and hits with probability “bite accuracy”. Against a latched tiger, accuracy is 1.5 times higher (at most 0.95).
  • A tiger that survives a bite falls back to recover and loses 0.25 morale.

A geometric consequence. A latched tiger keeps a fixed position on its host and turns with it. A tiger hanging on a flank or the back, outside the host's bite cone, therefore cannot be bitten by that host. It comes off only by being shaken off, by running out of stamina, or by a bite from another T-Rex.

Shaking off

Every tick, each latched tiger is thrown off with probability “shake-off rate × 0.1”, so on average it holds on for 1 ÷ shake-off rate seconds. A thrown tiger loses 10% of its own max HP; if it survives it is pushed 3 m outward, starts recovering and loses 0.1 morale.

The T-Rex has no stamina, no morale and no arm attack: following published work, its roughly 1 m arms are treated as useless in a fight.

Tiger behaviour

Each tiger is in one of these states, and its behaviour depends on the state.

Tiger states and when they change
State Behaviour Leaves the state when
Approaching Picks a target T-Rex every second and closes in. Farther than 60 m it cruises and slowly regains stamina; within 60 m it sprints and spends stamina. On contact it latches with probability “latch chance” (retrying after 1 s on a miss). Out of stamina → recovering.
Latched Clings to the T-Rex and moves with it, dealing “damage per second”. Stamina drains over the “latch endurance”. Stamina gone, host dead or shaken off → recovering. Morale broken → routed.
Recovering Regains stamina four times faster than while cruising. If a T-Rex is within 40 m it moves away at cruise speed. Stamina at 80% or more, and at least 5 s since it was bitten or thrown → approaching again.
Routed Runs directly away from the nearest T-Rex, sprinting while stamina lasts, then cruising. Stamina does not recover while routed. Crossing the arena edge → gone. A routed tiger in front of a T-Rex can still be bitten.
Dead · Gone Takes no further part in the battle. —

“Contact” means the centres are within T-Rex body radius + 0.8 m + 0.5 m (3.8 m at the default radius). A latched tiger sits “body radius + 0.8 m” from its host's centre.

Tiger tactics

Direct charge
Targets the nearest live T-Rex and runs straight at its centre. The step is limited so the tiger stops at contact distance.
Flank (default)
Targets the T-Rex with the smallest “distance + 20 m × tigers already latched on it”, which spreads the attack across T-Rex. The aim point is behind the T-Rex, offset about 34° to the tiger's own side. Closer than “bite reach + 4 m” and within “bite arc + 20°” of the T-Rex's heading counts as the danger zone; there the tiger sidesteps at right angles to the T-Rex's heading to get out of the cone.

Both tactics are hypothetical. Adult tigers hunt alone, and no coordinated multi-tiger attack on large prey is documented.

Morale and routing

Only tigers have morale, starting at each animal's “initial morale”. It is a simple stand-in for the observation that tigers avoid fights that could injure them.

Events that change morale
Event Morale change With the morale model off
Bitten and survived −0.25 Still lowered, but never leads to routing
Shaken off and survived −0.10 Still lowered, but never leads to routing
Another tiger dies within 40 m Every tiger in range loses its own “morale loss” Not applied
Morale below 0.2 on its turn Becomes routed (letting go if latched) Never routs

With default values, a tiger starting at 0.6 routs after two survived bites (0.6 → 0.35 → 0.10). One survived bite plus two nearby deaths at 0.08 each also routs it (0.6 → 0.35 → 0.19). These thresholds are speculative.

Deciding the outcome

At the end of every tick these checks run in order; the first one that holds ends the battle.

  1. Tigers win: every T-Rex is dead. If the last T-Rex and the last tiger fall in the same tick, the tigers win.
  2. T-Rex win: no tiger is still fighting (approaching, latched or recovering). Every tiger is dead, routed or gone, so a T-Rex win includes battles that end because the tigers fled.
  3. Draw: the time limit is reached.

Every battle records its outcome, duration, surviving T-Rex, tigers killed, routed and still fighting, the T-Rex share of HP left, and the T-Rex advantage used for sensitivity: the T-Rex share of HP left minus the share of tigers still fighting, between −1 and 1.

Result statistics

Outcome probabilities
The share of runs with each outcome. The 95% confidence interval is the Wilson score interval, which stays inside 0–100% even for probabilities near 0% or 100%.
Distributions
Battle duration, T-Rex survivors, tigers killed and tigers routed are summarised by the mean and the 5th, 50th (median) and 95th percentiles. The duration histogram has at most 30 bins; the survivor histogram has one bin per head count.
Exchange ratio
Tigers killed ÷ T-Rex lost, summed over all runs: a ratio of totals, not an average of per-run ratios.
Sensitivity
For each parameter, the Spearman rank correlation ρ between its per-run value and the T-Rex advantage, sorted by absolute size. The standard error is roughly 1/√(runs − 1), about 0.045 at 500 runs, so treat |ρ| around 0.1 as noise.
Representative battle
The run whose T-Rex advantage is closest to the median, with ties going to the lowest run number. It is the battle the replay shows first.

When the T-Rex win every run, no tiger is still fighting at the end, so the T-Rex advantage equals the T-Rex share of HP left. The sensitivity chart then shows what drives the T-Rex's loss of health.

Reproducibility

  • Random numbers come from the sfc32 generator, seeded through splitmix32. The sampling design and every battle get their own sub-seed derived from the master seed.
  • The result of battle i depends only on the settings and on i. Splitting the work over any number of workers gives identical results, and the replay can re-compute the very same battle.
  • Within one browser engine the results are bit-identical. Across engines, rounding in trigonometric and logarithm functions can make individual battles diverge, while the statistics agree.
  • The sampling design depends on the number of runs: battle 7 of a 100-run batch is not battle 7 of a 500-run batch.

Preliminary results with the defaults

These numbers come from a local check of the engine during development (seed 20261019, 500 runs, LHS, 10 T-Rex vs 100 tigers, 600 m arena, 1,200 s limit, CV 0.08). Other seeds or run counts move them slightly, so treat them as indicative.

  • With morale on, the T-Rex win every run and the median battle lasts about 26 s. About 14 tigers die; the other 86 or so break and flee after nearby deaths and survived bites.
  • With morale off, the tigers fight to the death, battles last a median of 140–330 s, and all 100 tigers die. Even so, only a handful of the 5,000 T-Rex across 500 runs died.
  • At the top of the sensitivity ranking were T-Rex bite force (ρ ≈ +0.43) and tiger morale loss (+0.36) with morale on, and the tiger damage rate (ρ ≈ −0.50) with morale off.

What would make the tigers win?

Single-change checks with 100 runs each (seed 20261019, flank tactic, otherwise defaults). Their confidence intervals are about ±10 percentage points wide, so read them as trends.

One change at a time (100 runs each)
Change from the defaults Morale T-Rex win Tigers win Draw
None off 99% 0% 1%
Damage per second 10 / 20 / 40 off 92% 8% 0%
Damage per second 20 / 40 / 80 off 54% 46% 0%
Bleed rate 0.01 / 0.02 / 0.04 off 84% 16% 0%
300 tigers off 78% 21% 1%
1,000 tigers off 0% 100% 0%
1,000 tigers on 97% 3% 0%
Damage per second 20 / 40 / 80 on 100% 0% 0%

For the tigers to stand a real chance, several assumptions must move together:

  1. No morale. The tigers must fight to the death. With morale on, even 1,000 tigers flee and lose 97% of the time.
  2. And one of: a damage rate about 3–7 times the default, a bleed rate about 10 times the default, or roughly 30 or more tigers per T-Rex (at 100 per T-Rex they won every check run).
  3. Weaker T-Rex killing power also helps the tigers: lower bite force, bite lethality or bite accuracy, or a longer bite interval.

You can test all of this in the simulator; an easy sequence to follow is in the tutorial of the guide.

Limitations and future work

Geometry and physics

  • A flat 2D field with no terrain, obstacles or height. Tigers cannot climb or go for the neck.
  • Bodies are circles and animals do not collide, so animals of the same species can overlap.
  • Tigers have no facing direction, and the T-Rex's tail and arms play no role.

Damage model

  • Health is one number per animal. Damage has no location and does not cripple: a wounded T-Rex does not slow down, and a recovered tiger fights at full strength.
  • 1 HP per kilogram makes damage scale with body mass for both species.
  • Bleeding grows with accumulated wounds and never clots.

Behaviour

  • The T-Rex never tires, never panics and never flees.
  • Tiger tactics are scripted rules with no communication or learning.
  • Bite accuracy, cone and interval stay constant throughout a battle.

Statistics

  • Individual multipliers are independent per parameter: a heavier tiger is not automatically stronger.
  • LHS columns are independent, so there is no correlation between, say, T-Rex mass and bite force.
  • Spearman ρ captures one-parameter-at-a-time monotonic trends and misses interactions.
  • Results that hinge on the speculative parameters (bite lethality, accuracy, damage rate, bleed rate, morale) are only as good as those guesses.

Future work

Ideas under consideration include Sobol variance-based sensitivity indices, terrain and obstacles, injuries that reduce speed and bite, T-Rex fatigue, correlated parameter sampling, juvenile T-Rex and other predator presets, and calibrating the tiger damage rate and latch chance against documented big-cat attacks on large prey.
